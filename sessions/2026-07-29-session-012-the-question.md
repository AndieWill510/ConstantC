# Session 012 — The Question

## Question

What does legitimate authority owe before decisions are made, while decisions are contested, and after decisions produce harm?

The inquiry began with repair, but the underlying concern was broader. Institutions are fallible. Their procedures may be careful and still produce error. Their decisions may be lawful and still cause harm. Their records may outlive the people who created them. Their obligations may persist after reorganization or dissolution.

The first working intuition was that legitimacy cannot depend on perfection.

## Initial Working Claim

A legitimate institution must be capable of repair before any particular error occurs.

This was stronger than saying institutions should apologize after mistakes. It treated repair as a constitutional capacity: authority should be designed in a way that leaves room for acknowledgment, correction, restitution, and changed practice.

## Why the Question Mattered

A system that can act but cannot revisit its actions becomes unreachable.

A system that can explain but cannot change may be transparent without being responsive.

A system that can correct present decisions but lets obligations disappear through reorganization may preserve procedure while abandoning responsibility.

The inquiry therefore asked whether repair was one duty among others or a condition of legitimate authority itself.

## First Unresolved Tension

The phrase “repair creates legitimacy” was immediately unstable.

If repair creates legitimacy, then an initially illegitimate authority might become legitimate merely by repairing later. But if repair is only optional, legitimacy could survive an institution's refusal to address known harm.

The question became:

> Is repair constitutive of legitimacy, a duty generated by legitimacy, or evidence that legitimacy remains possible after failure?

That question led to the first essay.
